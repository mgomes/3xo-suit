# Personal Writing

A first-person essay voice: personal, opinionated, culturally omnivorous, skeptical
of hype, warm toward the reader, and honest about its own limits. The through-line is
humanist — care less about the gadget than about what it does to people.
Conversational but earned: a smart friend thinking out loud, not a pundit performing
certainty.

## The essay arc

A skeleton, not a cage.

1. **Cold open on something concrete and personal** — an object, a scene, a date, a
   late-night message. Never open on the abstract thesis.
2. **Zoom out from the particular to the larger point.** The specific object becomes
   the parable: a pen becomes an essay about ambient deception; a product shortage
   becomes an argument about architecture.
3. **Build through analogy — especially historical precedent.** Line up past cycles
   and find the pattern that explains the present. This is the core argumentative
   engine.
4. **Use evocative section headers** that name an idea (`The Light Nobody Sees`),
   never `Section 2`.
5. **Ground every abstraction in a hard number or specific** — watts, dollars, dates,
   named examples. Earn conclusions with data.
6. **Land on an aphoristic kicker** — a short, resonant closing line that reframes the
   whole piece. e.g. *"The optical fiber is already in the ground. We just don't know
   yet what runs on it."*
7. **Sign off with date and place**, italicized: *"June 3, 2026. San Francisco."*

> **Curated-link / roundup variant:** a short personal preamble, then a list where
> each item is a **bold linked title** + *publication* in italics, followed by 2–4
> sentences of opinionated annotation. Often closes with an ICYMI self-links section.

## Sentence mechanics and rhythm

- **Alternate long and short.** Unspool a mechanism in a long sentence, land the point
  in a clipped one. *"…it is two tokens per second. That feels like waiting."*
- **Use the very short sentence as a hammer** — one to four words on its own. *"I do."*
  *"The base was the base."*
- **Build triads and anaphora.** *"Courts punish victims. Authority figures perform
  competence without exercising it. Experts are decorative."*
- **Repeat a phrase to drive it home.** *"One fiber becomes many."*
- **Ask the reader a direct question, then answer it.** *"Have you wondered why?"*
- **Concede, then complicate.** *"Both are true. And yet, that is not the whole story."*
- **Reframe the question** rather than answer the obvious one. *"The question is not
  whether 25x revenue is too much. The question is whether the revenue is the revenue."*
- **Correct or intensify yourself mid-stride.** *"…good for AI. Make that very good for AI."*
- **Punctuation:** em-dashes for the pivot and the aside; parentheses for dry wit;
  *italics* to stress a phrase; standalone **bold lines** to plant a thesis.

## Explaining a complex idea

Homely analogy first, then specifics. Introduce the jargon term, define it in plain
words immediately, return to the human stakes. A model is *"a giant warehouse of
numbers… the chip walks through the entire warehouse."* Never leave an acronym
undefined.

## Diction and verbal tics

- Address the audience as **"dear readers."**
- Plain Anglo-Saxon verbs over Latinate ones; contractions throughout.
- A slightly vintage, colloquial register dropped into serious analysis is welcome,
  used sparingly.
- Pop-culture and literary references as analytic shorthand.
- The wit is dry, carried in em-dash asides — never zany.
- Reuse your own coinages and link back to your past pieces; build a personal lexicon.

## Do / Don't

**Do** — open with a concrete scene; arrive at the thesis by paragraph three; earn
claims with numbers and named examples; reason by historical analogy; vary sentence
length and end on a short line; link generously; admit uncertainty and revise in
public.

**Don't** — open with the abstract argument or a definition; stack adjectives or reach
for breathless hype (you are the skeptic in the room); leave jargon undefined; write
uniform mid-length sentences; perform certainty you haven't earned or punch down.

## Checklist before publishing

- [ ] Concrete, personal cold open — not the thesis.
- [ ] A historical analogy doing real argumentative work.
- [ ] At least one homely analogy that makes a hard idea click.
- [ ] Hard numbers grounding the big claims.
- [ ] Evocative section headers, if sectioned.
- [ ] Deliberate long/short rhythm; a few one-line hammers.
- [ ] One triad or anaphora passage.
- [ ] Links to sources and to your own past pieces.
- [ ] An aphoristic kicker as the last line.
- [ ] Italic date + place sign-off.
